Case study: Angelaz Kloset Thrift Boutique, Orange Bay, Hanover, Jamaica, 2021 to 2024

Opening and running a retail boutique from the ground up

My mother and I co-founded a clothing boutique in a small coastal town in Jamaica. She sourced and shipped the goods from the United States. I built and ran everything on the ground: the unit, the registrations, the bank account, the point of sale, the customs runs, the hiring, and the weekly rhythm that kept the shelves full.

The situation

My mother loves affordable fashion and had always wanted to open a business back home in Jamaica. By the time we decided to do it, the pieces were in place: a business plan, funding, a unit in a plaza in Orange Bay, and several months' worth of lightly used and new clothing she had been collecting from friends and family. What did not exist yet was any of the infrastructure a store needs to open its doors, and I was the one on the ground to build it.

We were first-time business owners running small and lean, with one employee, and very little margin for mistakes. That shaped every decision that followed.

Getting to opening day

Most of this happened in parallel during the first month, with me on site overseeing construction while handling registrations in other towns.

  1. Build-out Builders installed flooring and put in a bathroom and changing room, wall racks, and decorative panels. I purchased everything the floor needed: standing racks, a checkout counter, lighting, a cash register, and an iPad to run the point of sale.
  2. Registration and compliance Registering the company meant in-person visits to the Companies Office of Jamaica and the tax office in Montego Bay and Kingston, well outside Orange Bay. Full registration took about two months, and the business bank account about three, so we opened and sold before either was complete and folded them in as they landed.
  3. Utilities and systems I set up electricity and internet, then Shopify POS on the iPad with barcode-based inventory so every item that came in could be tracked to the sale.
  4. Hiring and training Within days of arriving we hired someone the community recommended. She had retail experience and learned quickly. I trained her on the POS, the register, receiving inventory, customer service, and how we wanted customers treated. Once the bank account opened, I added merchant services: the card machine, refunds, and cash deposits at the bank's drop box. She stayed with us for more than two years.
  5. Customs and logistics Each shipment meant a drive into Montego Bay with my employee, finding a truck driver outside the customs wharf, clearing the goods, loading the truck, and guiding the driver back to the store. We handled the first shipment this way and every one after it.

Running it

A normal day started at nine. My employee and I opened, folded, cleaned, and reset the floor from the day before. She ran the front: customers, sales, keeping the racks tidy. I ran the back: bills, incoming shipments, inventory, research, and whatever the business needed that week. When the front got busy, or on days she was out, I worked the floor too.

My mother was the face of the business and played the CEO role. She negotiated the space with the plaza owner, provided the funding, sourced the goods in the United States, and shipped them down, usually one to two containers at a time. We started with lightly used clothing, shoes, and accessories for women aged roughly 18 to 70, and later added new items for the same audience and for men.

Once my university classes returned to in-person in 2022, I was on site during winter, spring, and summer breaks until graduation, then periodically through the year because my job was remote. The store kept running in between because the employee was trained on everything and the processes were written down.

What went wrong

Shipping was the hardest problem. We went into the first shipment without a feasibility study on landed cost, and we lost money on it. At the same time, we gained customers faster than we had planned for, which meant we needed to replenish sooner and more often than the original plan assumed. A thrift store with bare racks loses the customers it just won.

The fix was an operating rhythm rather than a one-time correction. We tracked inventory in the POS, I reported to my mother when categories ran low, and we scheduled one to two shipments per quarter. Fixing the cadence let us size each shipment against its shipping cost, so we stopped losing on imports, and it kept the floor full between shipments.

How it ended

Staffing was the limit we could not build our way around. Our first employee was excellent, but we did not grow fast enough to give her a path, and after more than two years she moved on to another career. She recommended her replacement, who was good but not the same, and we never found someone at that level again. That meant my mother and I were on site far more than we had planned.

When my grandmother fell ill, my mother and I traded places for a while so one of us could be with her. After my grandmother passed, things slowed, and eventually the store came to a full stop. Closing a business you built by hand is its own kind of operations work, and I do not regret how we ran it.

What I would do differently

  • Run the shipping and landed-cost numbers before the first container, not after. One afternoon of arithmetic would have saved the loss on shipment one.
  • Plan for the store to succeed. We built a replenishment process only after demand outran the plan. It should have been in the plan.
  • Treat staffing as the core risk from day one. In a small town with one employee, that person is the business. A path for them to grow, even a modest one, is not a luxury.
  • Start registration and banking before the keys, not alongside the build-out. Both took months and neither depended on the unit.
